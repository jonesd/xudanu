The Twelve Problems

How Udanax Gold solved hypertext's hard parts — and where a modern engine takes them further

In 1988, a small Autodesk-funded team set out to build Ted Nelson's deep hypertext system for real. The design — tumblers, enfilades, transclusion, unbreakable links — was decades ahead of what hardware and infrastructure could support. What the team produced, released as open source in 1999 under the names Udanax Green and Gold, was a set of solutions to twelve genuinely difficult problems. The web shipped the simple versions instead and won. But the twelve problems never went away: every document system that wants quoting without drifting, links without rotting, or history without archaeology eventually hits them.

This document walks through each problem the way a developer meets it: the naive approach, why it fails at scale, what the Gold team invented instead, and — where it exists — how a modern implementation (Xudanu, an independent open-source engine in Rust) extends the solution with machinery the 1980s didn't have: CRDTs, content-addressed storage, modern cryptography, and Bitcoin timestamping.

Problem 1

Editing Without Shifting Everything

You insert one character at the top of a book. Why should the other million characters care?

The naive approach

Store text as an array. Every insertion at position p shifts all n − p elements after it. Insert at the front of a large document and you touch the entire buffer: O(n) per edit, O(n²) to build a document by repeated insertion.

What Gold invented: the enfilade

A balanced tree where every node carries a summary of its subtree — the total width (character count) below it. To find or insert at a position, you descend from the root, subtracting widths as you go; only the O(log n) nodes on one path are ever touched.

Top: array insert shifts everything after it. Bottom: the enfilade touches one root-to-leaf path.

The trick isn't the tree — it's the discipline: the width summaries must stay exact no matter what happens below. That invariant is what makes everything downstream (positions, mappings, links) work without fixups.

Where Xudanu takes it further

The enfilade lives on (edition/orgl.rs), but the editing layer is a purpose-built CRDT — so concurrent edits from different machines converge by construction instead of requiring locks or a coordinator. Gold had no answer for simultaneous distributed editing; the modern engine measures ~80µs/op under concurrent interleaving, roughly 900× the throughput of an earlier operational-transform-style engine.

Summary
Naive O(n) insert → Gold enfilade O(log n) → Xudanu CRDT-enfilade: O(log n) + convergence by construction.
Problem 2

Comparing Documents Without Reading Them

Are these two 2-million-character documents identical? The honest answer shouldn't take two million comparisons.

The naive approach

Walk both documents element by element: O(n) to say "identical," and a full diff walk to find where they diverge — every comparison, every time, forever.

What Gold invented: crums

Each subtree carries a content summary — a crum (Nelson's word for a crumb). Equal crums mean identical subtrees, full stop: equality becomes O(1). And diffing descends only where crums disagree: O(size of the difference), not O(size of the documents).

Crum comparison: identical subtrees are never entered.

Where Xudanu takes it further

The crum is a BLAKE3 hash, computed incrementally and verified by test armor. Subtree crums power revision compare (jump to the first divergent chunk), the change-detection used by collaborative sync, and content-addressed identity (Problem 10). One idea, everywhere.

Summary
Naive equality O(n) → Gold crums O(1) equality, O(Δ) diff — same today, now with cryptographically strong BLAKE3 crums.
Problem 3

Addresses That Never Renumber

Insert a paragraph between section 2 and section 3. Now every "section 4" reference in the world is wrong.

The naive approach

Number things sequentially. Any insertion renumbers everything after it, and every external reference to a renumbered thing breaks. The web's answer — URLs naming a location — rots when the location changes. Either way, the address is a hostage to the content's arrangement.

What Gold invented: tumblers + between-allocation

A tumbler is a hierarchical address — "server".2.1.1004.15.42 — with one crucial property: between any two tumblers there is always another one. Need an address between [1] and [2]? Take [1,1]. Between [1] and [1,1]? [1,0,1]. The space is infinitely refinable, so new content allocates between existing content and nothing ever renumbers.

Between-allocation: the address space is dense; insertion never renumbers.

Where Xudanu takes it further

Tumblers are fully activated as xan:// URIs — server identity, region prefix, work, span, and a ?rev=N version qualifier on the query layer (paths stay purely positional — no span/revision ambiguity). Between-allocation (Sequence::between) is property-tested and backs region_insert_between. And every work's address carries its creating server's cryptographic identity, so addresses survive replication: a replica answers for the origin's address.

Summary
Naive: renumber or rot. Gold: infinite refinement between addresses. Xudanu: xan:// URIs with version qualification and replication-proof server identity.
Problem 4

Edits That Don't Chase Pointers

A million stored pointers point into your document. You insert one paragraph. How many pointers must you fix?

The naive approach

All of them. Every link target, every quotation range, every bookmark shifts with the insert, so the system must find and adjust each one: O(pointers) per edit, and woe to the pointer you didn't know about.

What Gold invented: displacements (wids/ispans)

Store edits as displacements — "everything after position p moves by δ" — attached to enfilade nodes. Positions below stay valid because they're interpreted relative to the displacement, which propagates lazily down the tree only when touched. Better: displacements compose — five sequential inserts become one composed mapping instead of five pointer-fixing sweeps.

A displacement carries the correction so pointers don't move.

Where Xudanu stands — honestly partial

Mapping composition and span migration cover the practical cases: links, transclusions, and annotations survive edits. Full wid semantics — lazy in-tree propagation, relative positions in every node — is tracked as deferred work (FR-64-D3), triggered if profiling ever shows migration sweeps becoming hot. Gold's solution was deeper than current need.

Summary
Naive: fix every pointer per edit. Gold: one composing displacement. Xudanu: span migration + composed mappings today; full laziness deferred with a trigger.
Problem 5

Scoping Without Scanning

"Everything in this project / collection / client's space" — without filtering the whole store every time.

The naive approach

Tag everything with a collection ID and filter on every query: O(store) per question, forever, and the tags themselves are a parallel structure that drifts.

What Gold designed: the prefix IS the scope

Because addresses are hierarchical, a scope is just an address prefix: region 2.1 is every tumbler starting 2.1…. Containment is a prefix test on the address itself — no tags, no scanning, and nesting falls out for free (region 2 contains 2.1 contains 2.1.5). The hierarchy prunes the search space physically.

Where Xudanu takes it further

Regions are live server semantics: works created in a region context allocate their addresses under the region prefix; containment runs through the Sequence space algebra (prefixed_by); region members list in address order; and — beyond Gold's design — write enforcement implements the permeability principle: reads and transclusions cross region boundaries, writes don't. One server hosts N isolated docuverses (classes, clients, experiments) with visible boundaries.

Summary
Naive: tag-and-filter O(store). Gold: prefix containment, nesting for free. Xudanu: enforced, algebraic regions with nested visibility.
Problem 6

Quoting Without Copying

The quotation that updates when its source is corrected — Nelson called this transclusion, and it's the economic heart of the design.

The naive approach

Copy-paste. The copy is dead: when the source is edited or deleted, the quotation silently stops being what it claimed. Every copy is also new storage — the same passage stored a thousand times costs a thousand passages.

What Gold designed

Content included by reference: a quotation is an address range pointing into the original. Stored once, live everywhere. Nelson's canonical demo was two windows side by side, the same passage highlighted in each, in different documents — one fact, many homes.

Transclusion: both documents point at the one stored source.

Where Xudanu takes it further

Transclusion is an inline element with per-span provenance and optional BLAKE3 content-hash pinning; cross-server transclusion fetches from the origin's public API and verifies the hash (tamper-evident quotation across trust domains). Two things Gold never had: frozen quotation mode (pin to a revision for citation-stability, with explicit re-fetch), and reference-over-copy suggestions — the system notices when you re-type existing content (within microseconds of six typed words) and offers to turn it into a live transclusion. The discipline becomes an interaction.

Summary
Naive: copies drift. Gold: live references, stored once. Xudanu: cross-server + hash-verified + revision-pinnable + suggested at typing time.
Problem 7

Backlinks Without Searching

"Who links to this passage?" The question that made the web one-way and the wiki a query.

The naive approach

Scan every document for links pointing at the target: O(entire store) per question — which is why the web never answered it at all, and search engines approximate it with crawls.

What Gold invented: end-sets

A link is stored once, as a set of ends — each end an address range in a specific document. Because the link is registered with every work it touches, each end can enumerate its links directly: O(1)-ish lookup from either side. Bidirectionality is a storage property, not a query.

A three-ended link: registration is storage, not search.

Where Xudanu takes it further

Five built-in link types (comment, reference, disagreement, quotation, see-also) with a type registry; gathered end-sets (one end holds many passages — Gold's FeMultiRef shape); links that can attach to other links (comment-on-connection); and link ends now carry their target's permanent tumbler address, so links survive replication and server boundaries.

Summary
Naive: O(store) crawl. Gold: registered end-sets, O(1) both directions. Xudanu: typed, gathered, link-attachable, replication-proof.
Problem 8

A Page That Isn't Ten Thousand Nodes

Tree nodes per character: 10,000-character page, 10,000 nodes, 10,000 allocations. The tree eats the machine.

The naive approach

One element per character — the "pure" fine-grained model. Correct, and ruinous: memory and constant factors make real documents impractical.

What Gold did: inline coalescing

Adjacent same-kind elements merge into runs. Steady-state text is a handful of nodes covering whole paragraphs; splits happen only at edit boundaries, and coalescing re-forms runs as edits settle. The fine-grained model survives logically while the physical storage is coarse.

Where Xudanu takes it further

Inline coalescing with transclusion-boundary awareness (a transclusion never merges into surrounding text — the reference edge is structural), plus chunk-level storage so runs serialize as blocks. The Coles-notes version: logic fine-grained, storage coarse, both true at once.

Summary
Naive: node-per-character. Gold: runs that split at edits and re-form. Xudanu: same, with reference-aware boundaries.
Problem 9

Syncing Only What Changed

Two servers hold the same 2GB docuverse. One paragraph changed. What must cross the wire?

The naive approach

The document — or at best a byte diff of serialized forms: O(document) either way, format-fragile, and meaningless across structural changes.

What Gold designed: crum-level diff

Compare subtree crums at the coarsest level first; descend only where they disagree. Sync cost is proportional to the difference, not the corpus.

Where Xudanu takes it further

Chunk-level crum diff drives revision compare (per-hunk author and time from provenance), collaborative state exchange, and the forensic chain checks. Content-addressed chunks mean the wire carries hashes first, bytes only for misses — the same idea Git uses for objects, applied to document structure.

Summary
Naive: O(document) sync. Gold: O(Δ) crum descent. Xudanu: hashes-first chunk sync + structural compare with provenance.
Problem 10

Identity That Can't Be Forged

"Is this the same passage?" — answered by the address, not by trust in whoever's serving it.

The naive approach

Identity by ID number or URL — minted by whoever mints first, meaningless across systems, forgeable by anyone who controls the server. The web's identity crisis in one sentence.

What Gold designed

Identity is content: the crum (hash) of a passage is its identity. Replication dedupes for free — two servers holding the same content agree it's the same content, no authority required.

Where Xudanu takes it further — this is where modernity pays

BLAKE3 content addressing throughout; per-passage authorship with Ed25519 signatures (who wrote what, character by character); server identity in every address (domain, or a key-derived ns- name — so even a server with no domain has an unforgeable identity); and the whole provenance record can be anchored into Bitcoin via OpenTimestamps, making "this passage existed at this time" checkable without trusting anyone's server. Gold designed verifiable identity; Xudanu adds cryptographic teeth and a timestamp floor.

Summary
Naive: minted, forgeable IDs. Gold: content-is-identity. Xudanu: + per-passage signatures, server identity in addresses, Bitcoin anchoring.
Problem 11

Fast Where It Matters

Users don't read documents uniformly — they work in one region for an hour. The machine shouldn't treat all bytes equally.

The naive approach

Uniform access cost everywhere: the passage you're editing is as expensive as the archive nobody's opened in a year.

What Gold used: splay exposure

Self-adjusting tree behavior: frequently-touched nodes migrate toward the root, so the working set enjoys amortized near-constant access while cold content stays deep. The tree learns the hot path from use itself.

Where Xudanu stands

Splay-style exposure exists at the Edition level; the modern addition is the reuse-detection index (n-gram + MinHash tiers), which lazily builds exactly where editing happens. Partial parity — an optimization with headroom.

Summary
Uniform cost → Gold: the tree adapts to use. Xudanu: edition-level exposure + lazy indexes where editing happens.
Problem 12

History as a Data Structure

Undo, audit, versioning, recovery — four features in most systems, one mechanism in this one.

The naive approach

Rewrite files in place and bolt on separate mechanisms: an undo stack, an audit log, a backup regime, a version-control side-system. Each drifts from the truth; none compose.

What Gold designed

Write-once storage: chunks are immutable, editions are trees of chunk references, history is the sequence of references — not copies. Undo, audit, and versioning become views of the same structure.

Editions as chunk-reference trees; history as the root chain.

Where Xudanu takes it further

The same write-once discipline, extended: a WAL between checkpoints, a tamper-evident chained security log, manifest history with hash-chained slots, garbage collection that preserves referenced history, forensic checks over the chain (FR-63), and the OTS anchoring from Problem 10. History, audit, backup, and timestamp-proof are one mechanism with four names.

Summary
Naive: four bolted-on mechanisms. Gold: write-once chunks, history as references. Xudanu: + WAL, chained logs, forensics, anchoring.

The pattern behind all twelve

None of the twelve solutions is "do the naive thing, faster." Each one changes the structure so the work disappears: summaries make comparison unnecessary, hierarchy makes scanning unnecessary, references make copying unnecessary, registration makes searching unnecessary, immutability makes repair unnecessary. Gold never fixes up data it can make structurally unnecessary. That is the through-line, and it's why the solutions still read as modern forty years later.
