Eleven Patterns, One Running System

The Xanadu pattern language — each pattern mapped to a live implementation in Xudanu

In The Pattern Language of Project Xanadu, Maggie Appleton reframes Xanadu: not a failed product but a pattern language — a set of design solutions that keep resurfacing across the web in partial, degraded forms. Her essay catalogs eleven patterns. This page is our response. To be precise about lineage: her catalog and this system share an ancestor — the Xanadu design — but neither came from the other. We built from the Udanax Gold codebase; she read the vision. What is new here is the mapping: her catalog, used as a checklist against a running system, pattern by pattern — and most of them can be clicked.

Everything below is live. Exhibits live on xudanu.com (read-only) and demo.xudanu.com (editable sandbox). Her essay's pattern descriptions are hers; the mapping and the implementations are ours.

Pattern by pattern

1 · Visible links running

Her critique: the jump link is a gamble — you find out where you're going when you arrive. Hover previews and unfurls only get us to semi-visible.

Every connection in Xudanu is visible before you follow it: color-coded by type, with a hover tooltip naming the kind of connection and its far end. The Spectrum Sentence in the Gallery of Unusual Connections carries all six link kinds on one sentence — hover any underline and you know exactly what it claims and where it leads. The open next step from her essay — previews that point at specific text in the destination — is where we're headed with span-level previews.

2 · Parallel documents running

Her point: quotations should sit beside their original context, making cherry-picking and misquoting hard.

The ⇄ control in the links panel lays any connection's ends side by side; the Five-Way Junction compares one connection's five named ends in a single view. Version compare does the same across time (see pattern 6).

3 · Transpointing windows running

Her example cases (LiquidText, Roam hacks) show parts of documents visibly pointing at other parts.

The Two Drafts exhibit: curved beams connect the same passage carried across two versions, and each beam follows its passage as either pane scrolls. Connections are drawn objects, not metaphors.

4 · Transclusion running

She keeps a separate essay on transclusion dreams — the same text, live in many places, permissions intact.

Transclusion is native, not bolted on: barred passages in the Gallery's Live Window are windows onto text that lives elsewhere and updates with it. Every transclusion carries provenance back to its source (who wrote what, at character level, cryptographically signed). See the transclusion engine.

On the worry her transclusion essay raises — that direct embeds would make "every document a sad compilation of 404s" — rot is what copies do, not what addresses do. A transclusion here is a reference the server resolves by address; when the source revises, the span migrates rather than breaks. Quotations that must hold still pin to a specific revision, hash-verified — and since every revision stays addressable, pinned windows cannot rot either. The only way to break a window is to deliberately destroy every revision of its source.

5 · Bi-directional links running

Her history of bi-directional links asks why seventy-year-old ideas still aren't on the web.

This is the core gesture: select text, click Link, choose the far end and the type. The connection is visible from both documents without either being edited. The sandbox walks you through it in under a minute.

6 · Version control running

Git made programmers expect versioning; readers and writers still live without it.

Every revision remains addressable; the History panel timelines them; any two versions compare side by side, with transclusion relations making the comparison structural rather than textual.

7 · Modular text blocks partial

Her essay turns to block-first construction here — and stops: this section is one of the stubs marked draft-in-progress.

Compound documents assemble new works from passages of existing ones — blocks are ranges of real documents, with lineage, rather than free-floating Notion-style blocks. The block-level authoring UX is younger than the machinery under it. This is one of the patterns we'd most like outside input on.

8 · Stable, universal addresses running

Her stub: the web addresses documents, not passages; link rot everywhere.

Tumblers — from the Gold lineage — give every position a hierarchical address. Spans migrate as documents are edited because positions are addresses, not offsets. Cross-server federation verifies content by BLAKE3 hash at the far end. The deep dive is Tumblers & Enfilades.

9 · Annotation running

Marginalia on the web remains an unsolved guestbook problem in her sketch.

Comments are typed links (Comment), so annotations are first-class connections: visible from the annotated document, attributable, and — for private notes — enforced private server-side. Detectors can watch a document and collect new commentary as it arrives.

10 · Multiple views, spatial arrangements partial

Graph previews are the weak version of the spatial Xanadu she sketches — and 3D flight demos are the disorienting one.

The perspective view arranges connected works as a calm, navigable landscape — no flying. We are deliberately conservative here; her critique of XanaduSpace's disorientation is well taken. The docuverse mini-graph is the small-scale version.

11 · Micropayments by design absent

The micropayments pattern closes her catalog — the part of Xanadu everyone argues about.

Xudanu implements the other half of that bargain: Transcopyright licensing (TCo) is native — per-work license metadata, compliance badges, attribution stamping on transclusions. The server never handles money; that is a hard design rule. Licensing and compensation are conversation partners, and transclusion keeps the provenance that makes the conversation possible.

An invitation

Her essay is marked draft-in-progress from pattern 7 onward — exactly the patterns where a running system has the most to offer and the most to learn. So: an open offer.

If you are reading this and writing about these patterns: pick one from the catalog — especially the unfinished ones — and we will build the next live exhibit for it, credited, on the sandbox. A pattern deserves a running example, and a running example deserves a critic. The GitHub discussions are open.
